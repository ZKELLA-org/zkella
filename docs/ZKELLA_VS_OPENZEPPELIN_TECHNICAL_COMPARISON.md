Where a fact was already established there, it is cited rather than re-derived.

## A. Executive Assessment

**1. The architectural split is real, not cosmetic — and it cuts both ways.** ZKELLA's interior `transfer`/`transfer4` path hides sender, recipient, and the transaction graph in a way OpenZeppelin's account-based model structurally cannot match, regardless of how mature its proof system becomes (Section B.1, C.1–C.3). But ZKELLA's own `shield()`/`unshield()` boundary is just as publicly exposed as OpenZeppelin's entire model (Section C.2) — this is the single most important nuance missing from any "note-based = anonymous" framing, and it should be stated plainly in ZKELLA's own positioning.

**2. No Critical or High-severity vulnerability was confirmed in ZKELLA's on-chain contracts.** The one Medium-severity, cross-confirmed finding — `compliance`'s sanctions-root rotation lacking the timelock/guardian pattern every other security-critical parameter in this stack already has (Section C.4, finding #1) — was independently found by two separate review passes without either being told about the other. It is concrete, fixable with an existing pattern, and the clearest pre-mainnet security priority.

**3. ZKELLA's trusted setup is the single largest unresolved item in this entire review — larger than any code-level finding.** The project's own build script says so directly: *"THIS IS A DEV CEREMONY... suitable for testnet/development use only"* (Section C.5, finding #10). This is a bigger gap than any smart-contract bug because no amount of contract-level correctness compensates for a forgeable proving key. OpenZeppelin/Nethermind's universal-SRS model (Aztec Ignition, 176 independent participants, already complete) structurally avoids this recurring per-circuit cost — a genuine, durable advantage for their proof-system choice that has nothing to do with their current non-ZK configuration.

**4. ZKELLA's performance claims are real and live-Testnet-verified — and so, now, are OpenZeppelin/Nethermind's, for all six of their circuits.** No comparable OpenZeppelin figures existed in their public record (confirmed twice) — so this review built all six of their actual circuits, proved them with their own documented toolchain, deployed their actual verifier contract to live Testnet six times, and measured every one ourselves (Section D.6). Result: OpenZeppelin/Nethermind's UltraHonk verification costs **~2.5–2.6x more Soroban instructions** than ZKELLA's Groth16 verification, confirmed live on both sides, and their proof is **57x larger** (14,592 vs. 256 bytes) across every circuit checked. Separately, ZKELLA's own `shield_batch(8)` was re-measured live rather than extrapolated: its real cost was found to be **358,803,366 instructions — 89.7% of the per-transaction cap and 61.9% of the separate, lower per-ledger aggregate cap** (both queried directly from Testnet's own resource-config ledger entries), materially worse than the local estimate suggested. Three parallel fixes were proposed, independently red-teamed by three further review passes before any code was written, then implemented, tested (all 128 existing `contracts/token` tests pass unchanged), and re-deployed to live Testnet for confirmation: **real cost is now 199,682,653 instructions — 49.9%/34.4% of the two caps**, roughly half the pre-fix figure, with `MAX_SHIELD_BATCH` unchanged at 8. Full detail in Section D.6/G.

**5. ZKELLA's defensible differentiators are narrower than its full feature list suggests, and that's fine — the two real ones are strong.** Shielded swaps (Section E.2) have no OpenZeppelin equivalent at all. A shipped, working SDK is a genuine advantage today, though a perishable one. Persistent indexing and "confidential DeFi composability," by contrast, are not differentiators a skeptical reviewer should fund — the first is a cost ZKELLA's own architecture imposes on itself, and the second is aspirational on both sides.

**Overall technical verdict.** ZKELLA is technically differentiated from OpenZeppelin/Nethermind in a narrow but genuine way — not a redundant reimplementation — and the differentiation is strong enough to justify continued, separate existence specifically for the use cases that need sender/recipient unlinkability or confidential swaps. It is simultaneously and verifiably behind OpenZeppelin on external security assurance (no third-party security review yet) and on trusted-setup maturity (per-circuit dev ceremony vs. a completed universal SRS), and ahead on transparency about its own real performance (OpenZeppelin publishes nothing to check). The right move is to preserve the core note-based architecture — it is what produces the actual differentiators — while treating the P0 list in Section G as mainnet-blocking, not optional polish.

## B. Architecture Comparison

### B.1 The one structural fact that drives almost every later finding

ZKELLA's note-based model and OpenZeppelin's account-based model are **not two implementations of the same guarantee with different cryptography** — they protect different things by construction, and no amount of proof-system maturity on either side changes that. Concretely:

- ZKELLA's `transfer`/`transfer4` entry points take **no Stellar `Address` for the note owner at all**. Spend authority is entirely the Groth16 proof; the contract's own comment states this directly: *"No `require_auth()` on any note owner: authorization is the proof"* (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs:598`). Sender and recipient are not Stellar accounts in the circuit's public interface — they're a nullifier and a transmission-key-addressed ciphertext.
- OpenZeppelin's `confidential_transfer`/`confidential_transfer_from` take sender and recipient as plain `Address` arguments, by design, documented explicitly on their own `privacy-model` page: *"It does not hide who transacts with whom, when, or which operation they call."* (verified live 2026-11-12, see docs/OPENZEPPELIN_BENCHMARK_COMPARISON.md and the fresh doc-page fetch this session).

This means the "account-based vs. note-based" framing used throughout this and the prior document is not stylistic — it is the actual privacy boundary. Section C's privacy matrix (produced independently this session) confirms this is the single largest, least-ambiguous divergence between the two systems.

### B.2 Side-by-side architectural comparison

| Dimension | ZKELLA | OpenZeppelin Confidential Tokens |
|---|---|---|
| State model | UTXO-style notes: `cm = H(H(H(value, asset), H(rho, rcm)), owner_pk)`, inserted into an append-only Merkle tree (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs`) | Per-account Pedersen-committed balance on the Grumpkin curve (`packages/confidential/`) |
| Spend authorization | A Groth16 proof of nullifier/commitment knowledge — no Stellar signature from the note owner (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs:598`) | `Address.require_auth()` on sender, standard Soroban/Stellar signature |
| Double-spend prevention | Explicit on-chain nullifier set, checked unconditionally on every spend | "Commitment chaining," no explicit nullifier/nonce, per OpenZeppelin's own `docs/protocol/security.md` (already verified in the prior document) |
| Public inputs at a transfer | Anchor (Merkle root), nullifiers, output commitments, value commitments, fee, asset id — no identity | Sender address, recipient address, encrypted/committed amount |
| Fee-payer / tx submitter | Decoupled via an optional relayer: `relayer: Option<Address>`, resolved and `require_auth()`'d *before* any state mutation (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs:674`), paid from the shielded pool itself (`RelayerFeeEvent`, line 869) | The transacting account itself signs and pays; no relayer/fee-abstraction pattern in the reviewed interface |
| Shield/unshield (boundary) | **Not anonymous.** `shield()`/`unshield()` take a plaintext `from`/`to` `Address` with `require_auth()` (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs:285,449`) and plaintext `amount`/`asset` public inputs — the classic shielded-pool entry/exit problem | `deposit`/`withdraw` are plain account operations with the same exposure — no architectural difference at the boundary |
| Composability across contracts | Cross-contract calls via thin, non-`#[contract]` interface crates (`token-interface`, `verifier-interface`) specifically to avoid Soroban's unconditional-export WASM-linker collision (`duplicate symbol: initialize`) — `swap` depends on `token-interface` to reuse `token::unshield`'s already-verified proof as its own note-ownership check, and on `verifier-interface` for VK lookups, rather than re-implementing either | Single-package trait (`ConfidentialToken`, 11 entry points) — simpler surface, no cross-contract decomposition needed since there's no separate note/verifier/swap/compliance contract split |
| Swap primitive | `contracts/swap`: commit/reveal escrow with a dedicated fairness circuit (`SwapFairness`) binding a revealed `(amount_out, min_amount_out)` pair to a hidden `intent_commitment` | **None.** Confirmed in the prior document's entry-point inventory — no swap, AMM, or exchange primitive anywhere in the reviewed `ConfidentialToken` surface |
| Compliance primitive | Separate `compliance` contract: Groth16 non-membership proof against a governance-set sanctions root | `clawback` circuit, baked into the token itself, plus per-transfer ECDH disclosure to a designated party |
| Delegated spend authority | None — ZKELLA has no equivalent to a "spender" role; closest analogue (viewing keys) is read-only disclosure, architecturally separate from spending | `set_spender`/`revoke_spender`/`is_spender`/`get_spender_delegation` bundled into the token contract itself |
| Client bookkeeping burden | Real: wallet must track notes, nullifiers, Merkle paths, and run an indexer (or trust one) to reconstruct spendable state | Minimal: balance is just an account's own committed value, queryable directly, no note-set reconstruction needed |

### B.3 Critical question: does ZKELLA solve a problem OpenZeppelin's system doesn't?

**Yes, for exactly one property, and the evidence is unambiguous: sender/recipient unlinkability for the interior transfer path.** OpenZeppelin's account model cannot provide this regardless of circuit maturity, because the two addresses are arguments to the function, not values inside a hidden witness — fixing their non-ZK verifier would do nothing to change this (see Section C). ZKELLA's `transfer`/`transfer4` is the only one of the two systems where this property holds at all, and even there it holds only for the interior path — not for `shield`/`unshield`, which deanonymize by the same mechanism OpenZeppelin's model deanonymizes everything (a plaintext, authorized `Address`).

For every other property this document examines — amount hiding, balance hiding, asset-type hiding on the interior path, compliance disclosure, viewing-key access — both systems solve a materially similar problem with different cryptography, not a problem the other one leaves unsolved. ZKELLA's shielded swap is the other clear case of solving something OpenZeppelin doesn't address at all, though that is a feature-surface gap (OpenZeppelin simply hasn't built a swap primitive), not evidence that one architecture is incapable of it.

### C.2 Discussion of the rows that matter

**Sender/recipient identity (rows 1–2) is the one place "note-based vs. account-based" is not a stylistic difference — it is the actual privacy boundary**, as established in Section B.1.

**The transaction graph (rows 5–6) is where ZKELLA's own privacy claim needs the most precision, and where overclaiming is easiest.** ZKELLA does **not** provide end-to-end lifecycle anonymity for a note — only for the segment spent entirely inside `transfer`/`transfer4`. The moment a note is shielded or unshielded, the party, amount, and asset are on-chain in the clear. This is the standard shielded-pool entry/exit problem: hiding the interior graph does not hide the boundary. Any observer correlating a shield of amount X at time T with an unshield of amount X (minus relayer fee) at T+Δ gets a timing/amount side channel regardless of interior cryptography. This should be stated plainly in ZKELLA's own positioning material, since "note-based = untraceable" framing from the broader privacy-coin space would mislead a reader into assuming more than this system delivers at the boundary.

**The relayer-based fee-payer decoupling (row 8) is a genuine, code-verified mitigation — but only for the interior path.** `transfer`/`transfer4`'s optional relayer closes the "whoever pays gas is probably the real party" correlation channel for interior transfers (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs:646-676`); it does not and cannot extend to `shield`/`unshield`, where the boundary-crossing party necessarily signs and pays themselves.

**The indexer operator learns nothing beyond what the public ledger already shows.** Both backends store exactly `(leaf_index, commitment, encrypted_note, ledger)` — ciphertext and commitment, never a decrypted field (`indexer/src/db-sqlite.ts:26`, `indexer/src/db-postgres.ts:42`). This is a correctly-scoped design, not a gap.

**OpenZeppelin's non-ZK verifier caveat is a separate axis from identity visibility and must never be merged with it.** Even a future zk-flavour verifier changes nothing about `confidential_transfer`'s two `Address` arguments being public ledger data. These are independent weaknesses on independent properties.

**Anonymity-set size is a usage statistic, not a cryptographic property, and both systems currently have a tiny one.** ZKELLA's Testnet deployment history shows leaf indices in the low single digits (`deployments.json`'s `_live_checks` note cites leaf 0 and leaf 5). No evidence of meaningful Confidential Token transaction volume was found on OpenZeppelin's side either. Neither system's privacy guarantee is realized at scale yet — both are privacy-*capable*, not privacy-*proven-in-the-wild*. This is a maturity/adoption gap for both, not a cryptographic flaw in either.

### C.3 Which model is stronger, for which use case

For **a payment where the two parties' identities and the fact-of-payment must not be linkable on a public ledger** (payroll, peer-to-peer remittance, any payment where a competitor or counterparty must not learn who pays whom) — ZKELLA's interior `transfer`/`transfer4` path is the stronger model, specifically because sender and recipient are not on-chain `Address` values and the relayer mechanism removes the fee-payer side channel too. OpenZeppelin's account-based design cannot match this regardless of proof-system maturity.

For **a confidential-balance token where the primary requirement is hiding how much an already-identified, already-KYC'd account holds or moves** (a regulated stablecoin where identity is already disclosed to the issuer/regulator and the only confidentiality need is balance/amount privacy from other market participants) — OpenZeppelin's account-based model is the better fit: simpler integration, no note/nullifier/Merkle-tree client bookkeeping, and its weaker identity-privacy is irrelevant when identity was never meant to be hidden.

**Finding added retroactively from Section D.6's build/deploy exercise, not from reading ZKELLA's own code — a comparative data point, not a ZKELLA finding.** Deploying Nethermind's own reference verifier wrapper contract (`contracts/rs-soroban-ultrahonk`) for the live-Testnet performance measurement in Section D surfaced its own doc comment, verbatim: *"This wrapper has no governor, no deployer auth, and no access controls. Anyone can deploy an instance with an arbitrary VK... Do not rely on the contract address alone as a trust anchor."* This is a materially more minimal trust model than ZKELLA's `verifier` contract, which gates VK registration/rotation behind governance's timelock (above). It cuts both ways: a system with no admin surface at all has no admin-key-compromise risk either, by construction — but it also means the burden of verifying which VK a given deployed instance actually holds falls entirely on whoever integrates it, with no on-chain governance guarantee to lean on. Noted here for completeness since this review happened to build and deploy it directly; not independently verified against OpenZeppelin's own production wrapper, which may compose it differently.

**Maturity signal.** Findings cluster almost entirely in `compliance` — the smallest, most recently split-out contract (its own module comment says "split out of viewing_keys," i.e. the youngest code in this stack) — while `token` and `swap`, the oldest and highest-value-at-risk contracts, show no confirmed issues and extensive self-documented attack-path closure. Fuzz coverage (`contracts/token/fuzz/fuzz_targets/`) spans all six contracts' admin and arbitrary-input surfaces — reasonable as partial evidence of absence for the input classes it exercises, but it doesn't reach the stateful, multi-call attack sequences (governance timelock races, cross-swap state interactions) that this review's own findings also don't touch; treat it as complementary evidence, not a substitute for sequence-level reasoning. The compliance/governance asymmetry (finding #1) is the one place the team's otherwise-consistent "find it, fix it, document why" pattern has not yet been applied.

### C.5 Cryptographic design assessment

**ZKELLA (Groth16/BN254/circom):**

1. **Soundness delegation (confirmed).** The Groth16 pairing check is delegated entirely to Soroban's native BN254 host functions (`env.crypto().bn254()`: `g1_msm`, `pairing_check`, Protocol 25+) — `contracts/verifier/Cargo.toml` has no `ark-bn254`/`ark-groth16` dependency, so there is no in-contract pure-Rust pairing implementation to review. Soundness rests on Stellar Core's host implementation, not on this repo's code — a real, confirmed dependency, and a future host-function semantics change is a direct risk surface ZKELLA's own team cannot unilaterally patch.
2. **Range checks are real, not a bare field-arithmetic assumption (confirmed, correct).** `circuits/common/range.circom:6-11` uses circomlib's widely-used `Num2Bits(64)` bit-decomposition — a genuine range proof. This matters because an unconstrained "`value ≥ 0`" check over BN254's ~254-bit scalar field would be meaningless on its own; the actual constraint here does the job correctly.
3. **A specific forgery class was found already identified and fixed by the team (confirmed, not a live finding).** `circuits/common/merkle.circom:17-42` carries an explicit comment describing exactly why `index[i] * (index[i]-1) === 0` (line 30) is required: without it, the Merkle-path "conditional swap" becomes an unconstrained linear interpolation letting a prover force any `root` from any `leaf` — complete membership forgery. The constraint is present and correctly shaped. Evidence of mature circuit engineering on the single bug class circom codebases are most commonly vulnerable to.
4. **Commitment construction (confirmed, standard, one unverified client-side dependency).** `cm = H(H(H(value,asset_id), H(rho,rcm)), pk)`, a 4-round Poseidon chain. Hiding depends on `rcm`'s entropy being genuinely fresh per note — a client-responsibility property not checked in-circuit (same as Zcash Sapling's `rcm`). **Inference, not confirmed this pass:** the SDK's `rcm` randomness source should be independently checked for CSPRNG use before this is called settled.
5. **Terminology correction, not a bug: this is Poseidon (arity 2), not the newer "Poseidon2" permutation.** `circuits/common/poseidon2.circom` wraps circomlib's original Poseidon (Grassi et al. 2019); the distinct Poseidon2 design (Grassi et al. 2023) is a different, newer construction. The Rust side matches precisely and says so explicitly (`contracts/token/src/poseidon.rs:1-8`: "t=3, nRoundsF=8, nRoundsP=57"). Both sides agree with each other, which is what actually matters cryptographically — but every reference in this repo and in `docs/OPENZEPPELIN_BENCHMARK_COMPARISON.md` to "Poseidon2" should be read as "Poseidon, arity 2." This correction should propagate back into that earlier document.
6. **A stale comment, not a vulnerability: no Pedersen arithmetic exists on-chain.** `circuits/common/value_commit.circom:5-6` claims "full Pedersen G1 arithmetic handled on-chain," but the actual check is a plain Poseidon hash, and a grep for `pedersen`/`G1Affine`/`ark_bn254` across the contracts found zero hits. Value conservation is enforced by direct arithmetic equality over private witnesses inside the circuit — sound and sufficient on its own, no homomorphic commitment property needed — but the comment should be corrected so a future reader doesn't go looking for Pedersen code that was never built.
7. **Circuit-type confusion — mitigated by construction, not by an extra check (confirmed, adequate).** `verify(circuit, ...)` selects the VK storage slot by `circuit` before anything else, so a proof is only ever checked against its own circuit's VK; cross-circuit replay would require one `(public_inputs, proof)` pair to satisfy two different circuits' relations simultaneously, which Groth16's construction makes practically impossible without an explicit additional domain tag being needed.
8. **Replay/malleability — prevented at the application layer, correctly.** Groth16 proofs are not malleability-hardened by the proof system itself, but every spend-type circuit emits the note's nullifier as a public input, and `token` checks/records nullifiers before accepting a transfer — resubmission is rejected at the nullifier-set check. This is the standard, correct mitigation (same approach Zcash-derived designs use).
9. **VK rotation is sound conditional on an assumption that was not re-verified this pass.** `update_verifying_key` requires admin auth and has no independent timelock of its own — its own doc comment says explicitly "callers should gate this behind a timelock... not call it directly in steady state." Safety is therefore *inherited* from governance always holding the admin role, not self-contained in the verifier contract. **Unresolved question:** whether any reachable code path can repoint verifier's admin away from governance to a bare EOA; if so, VK-rotation safety collapses to a single untimelocked key. Flagged for dedicated follow-up, not confirmed as exploitable.
10. **Trusted setup is explicitly a dev ceremony today — the project says so itself (confirmed, the clearest P0 item in this entire review).** `circuits/build.sh:1-10`, verbatim: *"THIS IS A DEV CEREMONY: single local contributor, `/dev/urandom` entropy. Every artifact this produces is suitable for testnet/development use only."* A production ceremony needs: (a) a phase-1 Powers-of-Tau contribution reused from an existing, widely-trusted public ceremony (the repo's own `build.sh` already references one for phase 1, so this part may already be reusable); (b) a phase-2, **circuit-specific** contribution chain with multiple independent contributors, each destroying their toxic waste, ideally beacon-finalized and publicly attested; (c) public verification transcripts; (d) critically, **a separate ceremony per circuit** — six for ZKELLA's current circuit count, and one more for every future circuit added. None of this exists yet. This is self-disclosed by the project, which speaks well of the team's honesty, but does not reduce its severity as a mainnet blocker.

**OpenZeppelin / Nethermind:**

11. **Non-ZK UltraHonk — the precise scope of what is and isn't guaranteed (confirmed, restated precisely).** Soundness and zero-knowledge are separate properties. The shipped non-ZK flavour can still give a real soundness guarantee (a cheating prover cannot get an invalid statement accepted, modulo the already-mitigated lookup-inverse leak) — but it does **not** give the zero-knowledge property: the proof transcript itself is not guaranteed to leak nothing about the witness beyond the statement's truth. For a confidentiality-focused system this distinction is material: soundness protects protocol correctness, but the missing ZK property is a potential privacy leak independent of whatever the public inputs already reveal. OpenZeppelin's own `privacy-model` doc page states plainly (confirmed via direct fetch this session): *"Whether other parts of a non-zero-knowledge proof... reveal private values has not been established."* — their own team has not proven the remaining witnesses are safe, only that one specific leak is closed.
12. **Trusted setup model — universal SRS, not per-circuit (confirmed from their own docs, a genuine structural advantage over Groth16's model).** OpenZeppelin's `proof-system` doc page (confirmed via direct fetch this session) states the Noir/Barretenberg toolchain uses the **Aztec Ignition** SRS ceremony: *"had 176 independent participants and is secure as long as at least one of them destroyed their contribution. Its transcript and verification code are public."* This is a universal, already-completed, well-attested ceremony covering any UltraHonk circuit up to its size bound — not a per-circuit ceremony needing to be redone for every new feature.

**Trusted-setup models, compared directly — the single most consequential structural difference this slice found.** ZKELLA's Groth16 construction requires a fresh, circuit-specific ceremony for every circuit, with the cost of getting it wrong (or not doing one, as today) falling entirely on the project — and this is *recurring* overhead: every future circuit needs its own new ceremony before mainnet trust is warranted. OpenZeppelin/Nethermind's UltraHonk amortizes this cost via one completed, well-attested, 176-participant universal SRS covering every current and future circuit up to its size bound. This is a real, durable advantage for OpenZeppelin's proof-system choice on long-term maintainability grounds — independent of, and not excused by, their verifier's current non-ZK configuration. The two axes (SRS universality, and ZK-vs-non-ZK) are orthogonal: ZKELLA should not be credited with "the harder setup problem" as though it bought something OpenZeppelin lacks, and OpenZeppelin should not be credited with "solved privacy" merely because its setup story is operationally lighter.

### D.2 The measured real/local delta, and why it matters beyond these two rows

The live-Testnet pass (full detail in the prior document's §11) found real Soroban resource cost running **14–19% higher** than the local `cost_estimate()` test-harness figure for the entry points directly checked against the network. The root cause was not isolated (candidate explanations: protocol-version/cost-model calibration differences between the local harness and live protocol 30, or footprint/archival-restoration overhead the local harness doesn't replicate) but the direction and rough magnitude were consistent across every check, including the full `shield_batch` sweep below. Practical consequence: every *other* row in D.1 marked "local only" (`shield()`, `transfer()`, `unshield()` as single calls) should be read as a demonstrated **lower bound**, not an exact figure, by the same ~14–19% margin confirmed everywhere else it was actually checked. The `shield_batch(8)` case was the one this review flagged as needing direct confirmation rather than extrapolation — and it has since been obtained, and fixed: the real pre-fix cost was **358,803,366 instructions, 89.7% of the 400M per-tx cap and 61.9% of the separate 580M per-ledger cap**, against a local estimate of 314.8M (78.7%, and the local test suite never checked the per-ledger dimension at all). That finding led to a full fix — not just a cap reduction — detailed in D.6: `shield_batch` now verifies the whole batch with one combined `verify_batch` pairing check instead of K independent `verify()` calls, and real post-fix cost is **199,682,653 instructions (49.9%/34.4%)**, confirmed live.

### D.3 Bytecode identity — why these numbers can be trusted as describing the live contracts, not a different build

`stellar contract fetch` was used to pull the actual deployed WASM for all six live `testnet_final` contracts and SHA-256-compared against a fresh local build from current source. All six hashes matched exactly (full hash table in the prior document's §11.1). Combined with the `git log` check confirming zero contract-source changes since the redeploy commit, this means the local measurements above describe the literal bytecode running on Testnet right now — the identity of *what* was measured is solid; only the *local-vs-live cost model* has the confirmed 14–19% gap described above.

### D.4 A proof-validity anomaly, run down and ruled not to affect the cost figures

Replaying a known-good historical proof fixture directly against `verifier.verify()` on live Testnet returned `Ok(false)` rather than the `Ok(true)` it produced historically, despite the on-chain VK, proof bytes, and public-input order all being independently confirmed byte-identical to what succeeded before. This was run down (transcription error, VK mismatch, and input-ordering error were each individually ruled out) and left as an open, narrowly-scoped correctness question — most likely a wire-format-encoding change in `verify_against_vk` since that historical transaction, not a live security concern — flagged as a follow-up item in Section G, not treated as settled. It does not affect the validity of the 33,450,026-instruction measurement: `Ok(false)` (not an `Err`) means the full deserialization and full Groth16 pairing computation ran to completion before the final equality check failed, so the expensive part of the computation is identical regardless of outcome.

## E. Differentiation Analysis

### E.1 Compliance and selective disclosure — maturity check

**Viewing-key scoping is all-or-nothing per owner, scoped only by time, not by asset or counterparty.** `ViewingKeyRegistry` (`contracts/viewing_keys/src/lib.rs:21-66`) stores exactly one `vk_commitment` per owner — no per-asset or per-counterparty key derivation exists on-chain. `ZKELLAAuditor.sync()` (`sdk/src/wallet/auditor.ts:68-86`) decrypts every indexer-visible note encrypted to the exported key from `birthday_ledger` forward, with no asset filter at the cryptographic layer (`transactionHistory(asset)` only filters the already-decrypted set for *display*, `auditor.ts:93-97`). The only real scoping axis is temporal, via epochs (`ZKELLAKeys.viewingKeyForEpoch`, `sdk/src/keys/keys.ts:138-141`; `rotateViewingKey()`, `sdk/src/wallet/wallet.ts:457-460`). There is no per-counterparty or per-asset disclosure grant on either side of this comparison — a shared gap, not a ZKELLA-specific one.

**Spend/view key separation is cryptographically real, not a naming convention (confirmed).** `nullifierKey` and `viewingKey` are separate, one-way, domain-separated KDF outputs from the same root secret (`sdk/src/keys/keys.ts:69-76,140`); `AuditedNote` (`sdk/src/wallet/auditor.ts:6-19`) explicitly omits the spend key, with a comment noting "computing it needs the nullifier key, which a viewing key does not hold." A viewing-key holder can read value and counterparty flow but cannot derive nullifiers — cannot spend, cannot even detect spends (receipts only).

**Revocation is a client convention, not an on-chain gate (confirmed, matches the already-established shared weakness).** `ViewingKeyRegistry::revoke` (`contracts/viewing_keys/src/lib.rs:50-61`) only removes the *advertised* commitment; its own doc comment states plainly: "Revoking does not change what a holder of an already-granted viewing key can decrypt." Forward cutoff is achieved purely by the SDK's epoch-rotation convention, which nothing on-chain requires an owner to actually use. This matches, and does not improve on, OpenZeppelin's documented retroactive-disclosure limitation.

**Sanctions non-membership is real verification sitting on weak governance (confirmed — this is finding #1 from Section C.4, restated in its compliance context).** `publish_compliance_proof` genuinely verifies a Groth16 non-membership proof before storing a record, but `set_sanctions_root` is a single, untimelocked admin call, and `governance.rs` has zero references to compliance (confirmed via grep) — `docs/GOVERNANCE.md:18` confirms this is a deliberate current-deployment choice, not an oversight, but it leaves the sanctions list one compromised key away from instant, undetectable replacement.

**ZKELLA has no native clawback/freeze — it reacts to the underlying asset issuer's, it doesn't perform its own.** No clawback function exists in `contracts/token`. `custody_shortfall()` (`contracts/token/src/lib.rs:1137`) detects when the underlying SEP-41 issuer has *already* exercised clawback externally, then shares the resulting loss pro rata across `unshield` calls and refuses any transfer carrying a positive relayer fee during a shortfall (`Error::FeeRefusedDuringShortfall`, line 670-671). This is, per the contract's own doc comment, "a deliberate acceptance of that issuer's clawback risk" (line 211-225) — not a limitation that was missed, a boundary that was chosen.

**No RWA-specific logic exists anywhere in the codebase** — RWA/stablecoin framing in project docs is positioning, not implementation.

**Institutional-adoption gaps, ranked by how directly they'd surface in a bank/regulator conversation:** (1) no timelock/multisig on `set_sanctions_root` — the clearest, most concrete fix needed, reusing governance's existing pattern; (2) no on-chain proof-freshness enforcement; (3) no per-counterparty or per-asset viewing-key scoping — the single biggest gap versus a real Travel-Rule workflow, where disclosure is almost always scoped to one flagged transaction or counterparty, not an entire account history; (4) revocation has no on-chain teeth, so no third party can verify rotation actually happened; (5) admin multisig is explicitly deferred as "a deployment decision for mainnet" (`docs/GOVERNANCE.md:57`) across most of the stack, but most material here given compliance's direct admin-key control.

**ERC-7984 compatibility — a direct no, not a hedge.** ERC-7984 is an account-based confidential-balance interface standard. ZKELLA is a note-based UTXO system with no per-account balance concept at the protocol level — there is no balance to expose through an ERC-7984-shaped interface without building an entirely separate aggregation/accounting layer the core protocol doesn't have today. This is not a compatibility spectrum; the data models don't line up, and "partial compatibility" would be a misleading hedge.

**Bottom line for the skeptical reviewer.** ZKELLA's strongest, least-arguable differentiators are **shielded swaps** (OpenZeppelin has nothing comparable, full stop) and **a shipped SDK** (a currently-true, though perishable, advantage). Its weakest claims are **persistent indexing as a differentiator** (a self-imposed architectural cost, not a gap in OpenZeppelin) and **confidential DeFi composability** (aspirational on both sides today, so it justifies continued investment in neither direction). Compliance non-membership proofs are real and differentiated but currently under-governed relative to the rest of the stack — a differentiator with an asterisk, not a clean win.

## G. Prioritized ZKELLA Improvement Roadmap

### P0 — Critical issues and mainnet blockers

1. **Run real, multi-party, per-circuit trusted-setup ceremonies for all six circuits.** *Complexity:* High. *Dependencies:* circuit freeze before ceremony start; a chosen coordination/beacon mechanism; public transcript publication. *Validation:* independent third-party re-derivation of each VK from the published transcript; confirm multiple genuinely independent contributors, not one party running multiple rounds. *Benefit:* removes the single largest unresolved trust assumption in this entire review (Section C.5, finding #10) — nothing else on this list matters if this isn't done.
2. **Commission an external, independent security review covering all six contracts, all six circuits, and the SDK's key-derivation/nullifier-handling critical path.** *Complexity:* High (external cost and timeline). *Dependencies:* `docs/EXTERNAL_AUDIT_PREPARATION.md`'s existing readiness plan; a stable code freeze. *Validation:* a published report with resolved findings, matching the standard OpenZeppelin's own "on-chain contracts and circuits are audited" claim is held to in this report (Section C.4/F). *Benefit:* closes the Security Assurance gap in the scorecard (F) — currently ZKELLA's clearest disadvantage versus OpenZeppelin.
3. **Route `compliance::set_sanctions_root` through governance's existing timelock/guardian-cancel pattern.** *Complexity:* Low — the pattern already exists for VK rotation and token settings; this is wiring, not new design. *Dependencies:* none. *Validation:* a regression test asserting a direct, non-governance admin call is rejected, plus a guardian-cancel test on a queued root change. *Benefit:* closes the one cross-confirmed Medium finding in Section C.4, and directly fixes the top institutional-adoption blocker in Section E.1.
4. **Confirm (or fix) whether any code path can repoint `verifier`'s admin away from `governance` to a bare EOA.** *Complexity:* Low–Medium. *Dependencies:* none. *Validation:* an explicit invariant test asserting admin can only ever be `governance`'s address, or a documented, deliberate exception if one is found to be needed. *Benefit:* closes the "inherited, not self-contained" VK-rotation safety gap identified in Section C.5, finding #9.
5. **Resolved and fixed during this review.** `shield_batch(8)`'s real Testnet instruction cost was found to be 358,803,366 — 89.7% of the 400M per-transaction cap, and, from a corrected resource model queried directly from Testnet's own `ConfigSettingContractComputeV0`/`ConfigSettingContractParallelComputeV0` ledger entries, **61.9% of the separate, lower 580M per-ledger aggregate cap** — a single transaction consuming most of an entire ledger's Soroban throughput, not just a tight personal margin. Three independent fork investigations proposed a fix (switch `shield_batch` from K independent `verify()` calls to one combined `verify_batch()` pairing check, already implemented elsewhere in this codebase); three further independent review passes red-teamed that proposal before any code was written, confirming the cryptographic core was sound (K=1 provably equivalent to `verify()`, no cancellation-forgery path) while catching two real issues — a silent loss of `verify()`'s VK-retention-window fallback, and an intra-batch duplicate-commitment gap if the in-memory dedup check wasn't wired correctly — plus a separate design flaw in a secondary proposal (naively making the batch cap governance-settable would risk an out-of-bounds array write, since the cap sizes a compile-time stack array). The fix was then implemented with both issues addressed (a fallback to per-item `verify()` when the combined check fails; a per-item in-memory duplicate check during collection), and validated: all 128 of `contracts/token`'s existing tests pass unchanged (including the exact tests that exercise intra-batch duplicates and the VK-rotation fallback), and the real fix was deployed fresh to live Testnet and re-measured. **Confirmed real result: `shield_batch(8)` now costs 199,682,653 instructions — 49.9% of the per-tx cap and 34.4% of the per-ledger cap**, roughly half the pre-fix live cost. `MAX_SHIELD_BATCH` stays at 8 — the fix made the existing cap safe rather than requiring it to shrink. *Benefit realized:* closed what was the single largest unquantified mainnet-budget risk in this review, with a real, live-measured, test-covered fix rather than a policy workaround.

## H. Independent Final Verdict

### Strategic option recommendation (Options A/B/C)

**Option C — a hybrid, complementary positioning — is the technically defensible recommendation, not Option A or B in isolation.** ZKELLA's note-based architecture is the only one of the two that can deliver sender/recipient/graph unlinkability and a shielded-swap primitive (Section B.1, E.2) — no amount of adopting OpenZeppelin's components changes that, since the gap is architectural, not a maturity difference (Option B alone would mean giving up the actual differentiators). But treating ZKELLA as a wholesale OpenZeppelin replacement (pure Option A, change nothing) ignores a real, structural disadvantage: Groth16's per-circuit trusted-setup cost (Section C.5) is a recurring tax OpenZeppelin's universal-SRS model doesn't pay, and ZKELLA currently has no external security review where OpenZeppelin has one (with caveats). The technically coherent position is: **preserve ZKELLA's core architecture for the use cases that specifically need its differentiators (unlinkable transfers, confidential swaps), while treating OpenZeppelin's account-based model as the right choice for the narrower confidential-balance use case it's built for — not as competing claims on the same ground.** Concretely reusable from OpenZeppelin/Nethermind without sacrificing ZKELLA's differentiators: the universal-SRS proof-system pattern for any *future* circuit (P3 item 2), and OpenZeppelin's governance/timelock documentation conventions as a model for closing ZKELLA's own compliance-governance gap (P0 item 3) — both are adoptable at effectively zero cost to ZKELLA's distinctive functionality, since neither requires touching the note/nullifier/swap core.

### Direct answers

**1. Is ZKELLA technically differentiated from OpenZeppelin/Nethermind?** Yes. Two capabilities — interior sender/recipient/graph unlinkability, and shielded swaps — are architecturally unavailable in OpenZeppelin's account-based model and are not merely less mature there; they are absent by design (Sections B.1, B.3, E.2).

**2. Are the differences substantial enough to justify a separate protocol?** Yes, for any deployment that actually needs those two capabilities. No, as a goal in itself — if a given use case only needs balance/amount confidentiality on an already-identified account, reimplementing that need in a note-based system adds complexity (client-side note/nullifier bookkeeping, a required indexer) without adding a corresponding benefit, and OpenZeppelin's simpler, now-externally-reviewed model is the better engineering choice for that narrower case.

**3. Which implementation currently provides better privacy, and under which assumptions?** ZKELLA, for sender/recipient/transaction-graph privacy on the interior `transfer`/`transfer4` path — an assumption-free win, since OpenZeppelin's model doesn't attempt this at all. Neither, for the shield/unshield boundary — both are equally exposed there, under the shared assumption that boundary crossings are inherently correlatable. Neither, for real-world anonymity-set size today — both are low-usage systems whose cryptographic privacy capacity is unproven in practice (Section C.1).

**4. Which implementation has stronger demonstrated security assurance?** OpenZeppelin, on points — a real external security review of on-chain contracts and circuits exists (by their own account), against ZKELLA's zero external security reviews to date, even though this review found no Critical/High ZKELLA finding and OpenZeppelin's own review carries an unresolved commit-provenance discrepancy (Section C.4, F).

**5. Which architecture is more efficient under comparable workloads?** ZKELLA, now directly measured rather than inferred. OpenZeppelin published no performance figures themselves, so this review built, proved, deployed, and measured their actual verifier on live Testnet (Section D.6): OpenZeppelin/Nethermind's UltraHonk verification costs ~2.5x more Soroban instructions than ZKELLA's Groth16 verification (83–87M vs. 28–33M, both live-confirmed), and its proof is 57x larger (14,592 vs. 256 bytes). Both proof systems were confirmed to share the same succinctness property — constant verification cost regardless of circuit size — so this is a real magnitude difference, not an artifact of one system scaling worse with complexity than the other.

**6. ZKELLA's three most serious weaknesses:** (a) a self-admitted, dev-only trusted setup for all six circuits — the largest blocker in this entire review; (b) zero external third-party security review to date; (c) `compliance`'s sanctions-root governance gap — the one place the project's otherwise-consistent "find it, fix it, document why" pattern wasn't applied.

**7. ZKELLA's three strongest innovations/differentiators:** (a) shielded swaps, with no OpenZeppelin equivalent at all; (b) genuine sender/recipient/transaction-graph unlinkability on interior transfers, architecturally impossible for an account-based model regardless of its proof-system maturity; (c) a shipped, working client SDK today, against OpenZeppelin's confirmed-unshipped one.

**8. Should ZKELLA change its architecture or preserve its current design?** Preserve the core note-based, nullifier-driven architecture — it is the source of both real differentiators found in this review. Do not adopt OpenZeppelin's account-based model wholesale; that would discard exactly what makes ZKELLA worth maintaining separately. Do selectively adopt operationally-cheap practices (universal-SRS tooling for future circuits, governance-pattern consistency) where doing so costs nothing architecturally.

**9. What exactly must happen before ZKELLA is safe for mainnet deployment?** Four of the original five P0 items in Section G remain: real multi-party per-circuit trusted-setup ceremonies for all six circuits; an independent security review covering contracts, circuits, and SDK critical paths; closing the compliance sanctions-root governance gap; and confirming the verifier-admin-repointing question is closed. The fifth — `shield_batch(8)`'s real cost — is now resolved: it was live-measured (358.8M/89.7%, worse than feared once the per-ledger cap was accounted for), fixed via a `verify_batch`-based refactor that was itself independently reviewed before being written, and re-confirmed live at 199.7M/49.9%. None of the remaining four are optional polish — each is independently disqualifying for mainnet on its own.

**10. What is the strongest technically defensible positioning for ZKELLA within the Stellar ecosystem?** Not "a better confidential token than OpenZeppelin's" — that framing invites a comparison ZKELLA doesn't need to win and currently can't on review/setup maturity grounds. The defensible position is narrower and stronger: **the privacy layer for Stellar flows that specifically require sender/recipient/transaction-graph unlinkability or confidential swaps — used alongside, not instead of, OpenZeppelin's account-based confidentiality for flows that only need balance/amount privacy on an already-identified account.** This framing is honest about what each system actually does, survives this review's own scrutiny, and doesn't require OpenZeppelin's existence to be a threat rather than a complement.
